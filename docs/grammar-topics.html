How to say because, so and therefore in Chinese

Chinese states cause and result with paired connectors, and unlike English it usually keeps both halves of the pair.

How to say but, although and however in Chinese

Where English drops one half ("although it hurt, I went"), Chinese normally keeps both: 虽然 … 但是 ….

How to say if, only if and no matter what in Chinese

Conditions in Chinese come as pairs too: the condition word opens the first clause, and 就 or 才 or 都 answers it in the second.

How to say both, also and not only in Chinese

Adding a second thing usually needs a marker before the verb — 也, 又, 都 — not just a comma.

How to say first, then and when in Chinese

Chinese puts time in order of what happened first, and marks the sequence explicitly with 先, 然后, 一…就.

了, 着 and 过: talking about completed and ongoing actions

Chinese has no verb tenses. These particles mark whether an action is finished, still going, or has been experienced.

Chinese complements: saying how well and with what result

A complement rides behind the verb to say how the action turned out — how well, how much, whether it worked at all.

把 and 被: doing something to an object

把 moves the object in front of the verb to stress what happens to it; 被 flips it round to say who it happened to.

Describing things in Chinese: 很, 太 and degree

Chinese adjectives work as verbs — you don't need 是. But a bare adjective sounds like a comparison, so a degree word comes along.

How to compare two things in Chinese

比 does the work that -er and more do in English, and the adjective never changes form.

How to ask questions in Chinese

Chinese doesn't reorder the sentence to ask something. You add 吗, repeat the verb, or drop in a question word where the answer would go.

Want to, can, should: Chinese modal verbs

会, 能 and 可以 all become "can" in English but mean different things — learned skill, physical ability, permission.

Saying where something is and what there is

在 marks location and comes before the verb, not after it — the opposite of English word order.

Being polite in Chinese: 请, 吧 and softening

Chinese softens requests with particles and set phrases rather than with conditional verb forms.
